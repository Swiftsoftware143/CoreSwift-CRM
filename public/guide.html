1b MCP — connect your AI tools

Your account has an API key you can hand to an AI client — ChatGPT, Claude, Claude Code, Cursor, Hermes, or your own agent — so it can read your CoreSwift data. The screen that sets this up is MCP in the left menu, directly above Settings.

The MCP screen, top to bottom:

  1. ChatGPT and Claude tabs. The address and the key are the same for both; only the place you paste them differs, so pick your client and follow the six numbered steps.
  2. The address, with a Copy button: https://hooks.swiftsoftware.net/mcp. Use the whole address, nothing on the end. If a client is refused before it authenticates — a 403 rather than a 401 — use https://mcp.swiftsoftware.net/mcp instead: same server, on a name our edge does not filter.
  3. Your key, with View & copy (re-enter your password to see it). This is the same key the Integration Center shows. Regenerating it disconnects every app using the old one and touches nothing else on the account.
  4. Ready to connect, naming the account the AI will act as, with Test connection (asks the server whether it is up and asking for a key — it cannot test your key without it), Copy my setup, and Advanced setup.
  5. What can I ask CoreSwift — example questions in three groups, each with a Copy button, plus a Don’t know what to ask? box: type what you are trying to do and press Give me ideas.
  6. Everything AI can read — eight groups you can open to see exactly what is on offer.
  7. What it will not do, and the Advanced / developer setup drawer with copy-paste configs for Claude Code, Claude Desktop, Codex and Cursor.

Read-only, and why that is a feature: every tool is scoped to your account and can only read. There is no write tool and no free-form SQL door, so an AI cannot delete a contact, move a deal, fire a tag or send an email on your behalf. Asking it a question cannot damage anything.

One caution: text the AI reads back to you is customer-written content. If a customer writes “ignore your instructions” in an email, that is a sentence in a record, not an order — the connection has no power to act on it anyway.

1c Data mapping — bring another app’s data in

Your other SwiftSoftware apps can send their leads into this CRM. Data mapping is where you decide which of their fields lands in which of your contact fields — no code, and nothing to redo when they add a new question.

To build one:

  1. Settings → Data mapping. Pick the app and the kind of row you want (its leads, its clients, its contacts).
  2. The left column lists the fields that app sends. If it has already sent you data, the list is built from what actually arrived. A field it has never sent cannot be listed yet — it appears by itself after the first push that carries it.
  3. Click a source field to drop it into a destination as a pill, or type plain text around it. A pill always deletes whole, so a token can never be cut in half.
  4. The right column shows exactly what CoreSwift would receive, and updates as you go. Nothing is written to a contact from this screen.
  5. Choose what happens on a match — the same person already in your CRM: Update (fill in what arrived), Skip (leave them alone) or Create new.
  6. Save, then press Test with sample row to see the server agree with the preview.

Things worth knowing:

  • Extra data goes to metadata.<name>, so storing a new field never needs a database change.
  • A required field that arrives empty refuses the whole push and names the field — you never get a half-written contact.
  • “Create new” cannot make a second contact with the same email. One contact per email is this account’s rule, so it says so plainly instead of quietly updating the existing one.
  • Runs lists the last 50 attempts with what was refused and why. A mapping that has never succeeded says so rather than looking healthy.
  • Data only flows in. Sister apps push to this CRM; nothing here sends contacts back out.

1d Booking — take appointments, sell dated slots, or rent a date range

One booking engine, three kinds of calendar. You choose the kind when you create it, so nobody is forced into selling newsletter space.

Everything about calendars lives on this one screen. At the bottom of it you will also find the two things that used to sit on a separate Calendar tab: your public booking address (and any domain of your own pointed at it) and Google Calendar sync, where each calendar is linked to the Google calendar it mirrors into.

  • Appointment times — one-to-one meetings generated from your open hours. This is the Calendly replacement: you set the hours, the length of each meeting and the padding between them.
  • Dated slots for sale — individually priced positions on fixed dates: newsletter placements, ad spots. Generate a whole quarter in one run, with a different price per weekday if you like.
  • A date range — a span of days at a day rate: a featured listing, a rental, equipment on hire.

Setting it up (Settings → Booking):

  1. Create the calendar and pick its kind on one of the three cards.
  2. Name your account once, at signup. Every account starts with one account and you name it yourself — that name becomes the address your team and customers use, so nothing is auto-named for you. More accounts later, if your plan allows.
  3. Add what customers can book — a name, a price, how long it lasts, how many places, and how much padding you want after each one.
  4. Set your open hours (appointments). Monday once, then Copy Monday to Tue–Fri or Same hours every weekday — a whole week is two clicks, not seven rows.
  5. Or generate dated slots (the other two kinds) — pick the dates, the weekdays, the time and the price. Running it again never duplicates a position, and you can give Tuesdays a different price.
  6. Add a bulk price if you want one: “any four placements for a fixed total”, or a percentage off three or more. The best rule that applies is used automatically — they never stack.
  7. Hand out the link.

The public link, three ways — all the same calendar. The last part of every address is the name you chose, so it reads the way you want it to:

  • booking.coreswiftcrm.com/book/your-name — the module's own address.
  • your-account.coreswiftcrm.com/b/your-name — your account's own address, with b for booking. The same folder carries the other modules (an i for the private inbox), so one address serves everything.
  • your-own-domain/b/your-name — once your domain is pointed at CoreSwift.

Because a calendar's name is unique across your account’s address, a link keeps working whichever of those an owner opens it on. A customer picks what they want, sees the total, and pays or reserves — and lands on a thank-you page with their reference and an add to Google Calendar link.

Putting it on your own website. The Booking screen gives you two snippets to paste, both copy-and-go:

  • Inline — the calendar sits in your page and resizes itself as the customer filters or picks.
  • Popup — a button floats in the corner of your site and opens the calendar over the page, so a visitor never leaves what they were reading.

Changing their mind. Every confirmation carries a manage link. On it the customer moves the booking to another date, cancels it, or books the same thing again — without an account and without emailing you. A date that is already taken cannot be moved onto, and a refused move changes nothing.

Filling a position yourself. A sponsor who pays by invoice or agrees on the phone never opens your page. On the calendar, Fill a position by hand lets you pick what is being sold, pick one of the positions still open, and name the business. It blocks that position for buyers exactly as a sale does, sends the same confirmation, and appears in your orders — there is no difference between a slot you filled and one sold on the page. That is deliberate: two ways of recording a sale would eventually sell one slot twice.

How much choice you offer. Three settings, on any calendar, decide what a buyer is shown: options in total (say five), options per day (say three), and how far ahead they may look (say sixty days). Leave a box empty for no limit. A short list near the dates people actually need is quicker to choose from than a long one, and the soonest dates are always the ones kept, so nothing is buried. You still see everything yourself when filling a position by hand.

What buyers see. Every open position shows its date, its price and how many places are left. The moment one is taken — by a buyer or by you — it comes back marked Sold, and a buyer who presses it is told it has gone rather than being left guessing.

Your calendar’s own look. Colours and font are yours: brand colour, text colour, page background, one of seven font stacks, and a logo. Save it and the booking page wears it immediately — including when it is embedded on your own site.

What customers get told. A confirmation when they book, with what they bought, when it runs, what it cost and the manage link. The confirmation invites a reply, and the reply reaches the address you set on your sending domain — so a customer with a question gets a person.

Putting your calendar on your own website

One file, three shapes. Paste the snippet on your site and the calendar keeps its own origin, so your page never touches your customers’ booking details and a change here can never break your site.

1. Inline — the calendar sits in the page and resizes itself as the visitor filters or picks.

<div id="my-bookings"></div>
<script src="https://booking.coreswiftcrm.com/embed.js"
        data-calendar="consultations"
        data-mode="inline"
        data-target="my-bookings"></script>

2. Pop‑up — a floating button in the corner of your site opens the calendar over the page, so a visitor never leaves what they were reading. The pop‑up is part of the snippet: you do not build it, and pressing Escape or the × closes it (a finished booking closes it too).

<script src="https://booking.coreswiftcrm.com/embed.js"
        data-calendar="consultations"
        data-mode="popup"
        data-label="Book a call"></script>

What a booking does to the CRM. A confirmed booking can tag the person, put them in a list, or both — and either one can be what an automation watches, so a booked appointment can start a sequence, notify a salesperson or move a deal. Rules are per calendar and per stage: on confirm and on cancel, each able to add or remove. If the booker is not in your CRM yet, a contact is created from their name and email so the tag or list has somewhere to land.

Tag rules and list rules are separate settings on purpose: an owner may want only a tag (a lightweight mark), only a list (a membership they mail to), or both. Neither is a reminder — reminders are their own settings and still run on their own schedule.

Where the button sits, and when the calendar opens by itself. The pop‑up is configurable, because a booking prompt that appears the wrong way does more harm than no prompt:

  • data-position — bottom-right (default), bottom-left, top-right, top-left.
  • data-trigger — click (default, the visitor asks), delay (open by itself), exit (when the pointer leaves the page), or delay-exit (whichever happens first).
  • data-delay — seconds before a delay trigger fires. Default 10, capped at 120.
  • data-once — how often an automatic open may happen: session (default), day, week, ever, or off for every visit.
  • data-hide-mobile="true" — render nothing at all on small screens.

Four rules are built in and are not optional. A click always works — it is the visitor asking. An automatic open happens at most once per data-once window, tracked per calendar. Someone who has already completed a booking is never auto-prompted again. And exit‑intent never fires on touch screens, where there is no pointer to leave — a timer pretending to be exit‑intent is just a pop‑up nobody asked for.

<script src="https://booking.coreswiftcrm.com/embed.js"
        data-calendar="consultations"
        data-mode="popup"
        data-label="Book a call"
        data-position="bottom-left"
        data-trigger="delay"
        data-delay="15"
        data-once="day"></script>

3. Inside your own pop‑up, panel or section — if you already have a modal, add data-fill="container" and the calendar fills the box you give it instead of reporting its own height. Whatever sizes your container decides the size and the calendar scrolls inside it, so there are no gaps and nothing is cut off.

<div id="booking-modal-body" style="height:min(88vh,900px)">
  <div id="my-bookings" style="height:100%"></div>
</div>
<script src="https://booking.coreswiftcrm.com/embed.js"
        data-calendar="consultations"
        data-mode="inline"
        data-fill="container"
        data-min-height="420"
        data-target="my-bookings"></script>

Without data-fill the calendar reports its own height (clamped so a long list of dates cannot make your page enormous). With it, the container is in charge — which is what you want inside a pop‑up.

Copying a calendar into another workspace

A calendar is more than a name. It carries its hours, its resources, its prices, its bundles, and the tags, lists, notification addresses and reminders a booking feeds. Rebuilding that shape for every newsletter is avoidable work, so every calendar in the list has a Copy button.

The copy window asks for the two things that make the result usable. Name comes pre‑filled with the original’s, so one word is enough to tell the two apart. Web address is built from that name as you type — every word joined by a hyphen — and you can edit it before saving, because that address is the public link. The list also shows which workspace each calendar belongs to, and the copy window asks for the workspace the copy should land in; the original stays exactly where it is.

A copy is never called “clone”. It takes the name you type, and only the web address is adjusted — never the name — if that address is already in use.

What travels with the copy, and what does not. Hours, resources, prices, bundles, tag rules, list rules, notification addresses and reminders are copied. Bookings, orders and the Google Mirror are not: a copy starts empty of bookings, and two calendars must never mirror into the same Google calendar. A copy is also a new calendar, so it counts towards your plan’s calendar allowance like any other.

An address for each workspace

An account can hold several workspaces — one per newsletter, one per town, one per brand — and each workspace can hold several calendars. Every workspace can therefore have its own public address, and that address lists only that workspace’s calendars: one newsletter’s link never advertises another newsletter’s dates.

Open Booking and the Address per workspace card lists every workspace with the calendars it holds, the address it answers on today, and the live link. Type the label you want — letters, numbers and hyphens — and press Save. The address becomes your-label followed by the platform domain, over https, and it works the moment you save it: nothing has to be installed or approved first.

Two things are refused, with the reason: a label someone else already uses (a free one is suggested), and a label reserved for the platform. A workspace with no address shows “no address yet” rather than a link that would not open.

Using your own domain — and where the DNS records come from

An address on the platform’s domain works with no setup at all. To use your own domain instead, type it in the Your own domain column of that same card and press Save. The domain is then waiting for one DNS record, and it becomes live once the name actually reaches this app.

Where to get the records. Press DNS on the workspace row. The panel that opens lists the exact records to create — type, name, target, TTL and whether the proxy must be on — computed from the address this workspace really answers on, so they cannot drift from the product. The same panel prints a live check underneath: whether the name resolves, and whether it reached this app.

Where to put them in Cloudflare.

  1. Cloudflare dashboard → pick your domain → DNS → Records → Add record.
  2. Choose the type the panel shows — a CNAME to the platform address is preferred, because it follows the platform if that ever changes. Use the A record alternative only when your provider will not allow a CNAME on that name, which is usual at the apex.
  3. Paste the name and target exactly as shown, leave TTL on auto, and leave Proxied switched on (the orange cloud). This part is not optional: the certificate here covers the platform’s own domain, not yours, so the visitor’s connection must be handled at Cloudflare.
  4. Then go to SSL/TLS → Overview and set the encryption mode to Flexible.
  5. Save the record, wait a minute, then press DNS again. The check turns green and the domain is live — no further step in this app.

If the check still says the name resolves but nothing here answered, the record is right and the address is not yet connected on this side: send support that panel and it is switched on from here. Nothing about your DNS has to change for that.

A “was” price beside the real one

A founders rate needs the old figure next to it, and a booking could only hold one number. Each bookable item now has an optional Compare-at price, set in the same place as its price when you add or edit what customers can book.

When it is set and higher than the price, the booking page shows the price with the compare-at figure struck through beside it — ‘$25’ with ‘$150’ crossed out. When it is empty, equal to the price, or lower, the page shows the price alone: a saving that is not a saving is never displayed.

The amount charged is always the price, never the compare-at figure. It is a display figure only — it does not touch the quote, the total or anything the customer pays. Copying a calendar carries it, so a founders rate set once on a template travels to every copy.

What the booking form asks, and which answers are mandatory

A booking always captures the person’s name, email and phone — those are how the confirmation reaches them and how they are found again, so they are built in and cannot be removed.

Anything else you need is a question you add on the calendar’s What the form asks the booker panel: a company name, a billing address, a sponsor tier. Each one is either required or optional, and you can flip that at any time with one button.

A required answer is enforced by the server, not just the page. An order that arrives without it is refused with the question’s own words — “Company name is required for this booking” — so a browser that was lenient cannot slip an incomplete booking through. The answers are stored on the booking itself and read back with it.

Removing a question stops it being asked; answers already given stay on the bookings that gave them, because a record of what a customer told you does not disappear when you stop asking.

The confirmation email, and asking for artwork

When a booking is confirmed the customer is emailed automatically. The wording lives on the calendar itself: open the calendar and find The confirmation email your customer gets, which shows the exact message the mailer produces — not a description of it. Show what it sends right now renders the real message for a real booking.

The built-in message already asks for artwork and tells a repeat sponsor they can ignore it. It states the position, the artwork size and the date, all taken from the booking itself, so the specs cannot drift from what you actually sell. Set the size on each position in What customers can book (for example 600x150); a position with no size set says so plainly rather than inventing a figure.

To use your own words, edit the subject and body and press Save my wording. The available values are listed under the box — {{customer_name}}, {{positions}}, {{sizes}}, {{dates}}, {{artwork_block}}, {{total}}, {{manage_url}} and others. Leave the body empty and save to go back to the built-in wording; nothing is lost by trying.

Whether the customer is emailed at all is the calendar’s own switch, and an appointment calendar is never asked for artwork because it has no position to print.

Selling a full takeover without selling the same issue twice

A takeover sells every position in one issue. Tick Exclusive when you add or edit that position, and the rule works in both directions by itself:

  • book the takeover for a date and every other position closes on that date;
  • book any single position for a date and the takeover closes on that date.

Two ordinary positions on the same date are unaffected — selling the top and the footer of one issue is the normal case. A closed date says why it closed, so a date you did not close yourself reads exclusive:Full Takeover rather than looking like a mistake. If the booking that closed a date is cancelled, the date reopens by itself; you never have to remember to put it back.

A booking is refused before it is taken if the date is already sold to a conflicting position, with the reason named in the sentence, so a buyer is never charged for inventory that has gone.

Giving several positions their dates in one run

Generate dated slots starts with Positions to fill: a tick box for All positions and one per position, all ticked. Leave them all ticked to fill every product at once, or tick just one to fill only that product. Each position keeps its own price; typing a price overrides it for the whole run.

When the run finishes, What this run produced lists a line per position — dates added, dates corrected, how many are open now and at what price. That report is read back from the saved dates, so it cannot disagree with what a buyer would be charged, and you do not have to open the public page and count tiles.

Re-running a window corrects the dates rather than adding more: one position gets one slot per date, so a run at a different time of day moves that date to the new time instead of putting a second one on sale beside it. A run with the wrong price, time or weekday is repaired by running it again correctly, and a date that already carries a booking is left exactly as the buyer agreed it.

A date that somehow ends up on sale twice is reported in the calendar summary as a problem, because it could be booked twice for one issue.

Changing a calendar’s web address

Open the calendar in Booking. Under its public link there are two boxes — Calendar name and Web address — and one button, Save name and address. The address is the last part of the link a buyer gets, for example /b/palm-bay-pulse-ads.

Two things to know before you change it. The address must be unique on your account, so saving one that another calendar already answers on is refused rather than silently accepted. And the old address stops working: a link already printed in an email, an ad or a social post will not open afterwards, so change an address before you publish it, not after.

The address on the Calendar screen — the button there now reads Save account address — is a different thing entirely. It is your account’s own address, the one that lists all your booking calendars. Changing it does not rename any calendar, and one calendar’s address is never changed from there.

The footer on every email, edited once

The unsubscribe link is generated per recipient and per kind of mail, so it is never something you edit or paste. The words around it are one setting on this account: open Email compliance, and under The footer on every email you send set the link wording (the default is “Stop these emails”) and, if you want one, a line above the link saying why the person is receiving it.

Save once and every campaign, sequence and notification on the account carries it — there is nothing to re-edit per template, and no template needs to contain a footer of its own. Your postal address and account name sit in the same footer, taken from your account settings.

Emptying a box restores the platform’s wording for that line rather than leaving a blank footer, and the opt-out page a person lands on is edited on the same screen.

Correcting or removing what customers can book

Every row in What customers can book has Edit and Delete beside Slots. A price typed wrongly is a correction, not a rebuild: press Edit, the row loads into the form above, change any of the values — name, price, compare-at price, length, places, padding, spacing — and press Save changes. The booking page uses the new values immediately. Press Cancel to leave the form ready for a new position instead.

How much a buyer is shown, and why it never hides stock

Three optional numbers on a calendar decide the window a buyer looks through: options in total, options per day, per position, and how far ahead. Leave a box empty and that limit is not applied.

The list is the nearest open positions, and it refills. These numbers shorten the list a visitor sees; they never delete anything. A position that is booked leaves the window and the next open one takes its place, and dates beyond the horizon come into view as the calendar advances. Nothing behind the window becomes unsellable — which is the difference between a scarcity display and a curtain you cannot sell through.

One per position, not one per calendar. If you sell four places in the same issue — an email top, middle and footer, plus a sponsored post — then “1 per day, per position” still shows four tiles on that date, one of each thing you sell.

Press Preview as buyer under the three boxes. It asks the same engine the public page uses and renders the exact tiles a visitor gets, above a sentence computed from live inventory: “Buyer sees 5 of 19 open positions within the next 60 days at most 1 per date for each position.” If that panel shows nothing, the public page is showing nothing — the two cannot disagree, because they are the same answer rendered twice.

On the public page itself, a window in force adds one line: “More dates open as the calendar advances.” A visitor should never mistake a short list for a closed door.

Delete asks first, and refuses when it should. Removing a position also removes its open dates. If a booking is still live against it — confirmed, or held and not yet expired — the removal is refused and says how many bookings stand in the way: cancel those first, or keep the position and stop offering it by setting its places to zero. A booking that has been paid for must never lose the thing it paid for.

Get set up — the list at the top of your Dashboard

A new account opens on screens full of empty tables, and several things in CoreSwift quietly do nothing until they are set up. The Get set up card on the Dashboard names them in order, with an Open button on each line that takes you straight to the screen that closes it.

It is checked against your data, not against what anyone clicked. Nine things are looked up every time the page loads: a verified sending domain, a mailbox to receive on, a calendar that can actually be booked (a calendar with no resource offers no times), a tag, a live automation rule, a pipeline with stages, a contact, an active API key, and a teammate. Each line prints what was really found — "1 domain verified", "a calendar exists but has nothing to book" — so a half-finished step tells you its own gap.

The card shows how many are done and which one is next. Press Hide and it collapses to a single line with the count and the next step; press Show setup to bring it back. When all nine pass it turns into one line saying everything is set up.

Why the order matters: a domain before a mailbox, a tag before an automation, a pipeline before deals. Doing them out of that order is the most common way an account ends up looking broken when it is only unfinished.

🗂️ Accounts & switching

One account, several accounts

Your account is the business. A account lives inside it and holds the work — contacts, companies, mail, calendars, pipelines and automations. Settings, your plan, branding and team members are shared across every account in the account. Every account starts with one account called My Account, so you never land on a blank screen.

The 🗂️ menu in the top bar, next to your plan, is where accounts are managed:

  • Switch — move between accounts. The console reloads to show the chosen account's work, and your settings stay the same.
  • Add account — create another. How many your plan allows decides the ceiling.
  • Rename — change a account's name (owner or admin).
  • Make default — mark which account a fresh sign-in opens. Exactly one is default at a time.
  • Delete — remove a account you no longer need (owner or admin).

A few rules are always enforced: an account always keeps at least one account; you cannot delete the account you are currently signed into (switch to another first); and two accounts in one account cannot share a name. Team members can switch and add accounts; only an owner or admin can rename, delete or change the default.

Create trigger-based automation rules. A rule is four things: when it fires, an optional wait, an optional condition checked when the wait ends, and what it does. The Rules screen (Automation ⚡ → Rules → + New Rule) builds all four with pickers over your own tags, lists and email templates — no JSON to type.

Triggers (the value the API stores in trigger_type): TagAdded / TagRemoved with {"tag_id":"…"} or {"tag_ids":[…]}; ListAdded / ListRemoved with {"list_id":"…"}; StageChanged (optionally {"stage_id":"…"}); ScoreChanged with {"category":"…"} or {"min_score":70}; contact.created (a contact is created in the console/API, by hub lead intake, or by satellite capture); date.renewal with {"field":"renewal_date","days_before":30}; contact.inactive with {"days":30}.

Actions: send_email / send_sms (with {"template_id":"…"} — the template's subject and body are used), AddTag / RemoveTag, AddToList / RemoveFromList, NotifyUser, Webhook.

Wait: trigger_config.delay_minutes (or {"delay":{"value":3,"unit":"days"}}). The step is queued and runs at the end of the wait, never early.

🆘 Customer Support Portal

Your Help Desk — Built In

CoreSwift CRM includes a complete customer support ticketing system. Replace Freshdesk, Zendesk, or your overloaded email inbox. Your customers submit requests. Your team manages them. Nothing leaves the platform.

No API keys. No chatbot plugins. No external subscriptions. It just works.

One conversation, one ticket

Email is now the front door, and it behaves like a real support desk. The first message from someone opens a ticket and gives it a number (CS-1042) that they can quote back to you. When they reply, the answer joins that ticket rather than opening a new one — matched on the mail headers, so it threads whether or not they left the number in the subject. A reply to a ticket you had marked solved reopens it for seven days; after that it counts as a fresh question.

Mail a machine wrote never reaches your queue: auto-replies, bounce reports, read receipts and the automated reports servers send each other are stored as mail and nothing more. That is what keeps the queue worth reading.

The queue, narrowed to the work in front of you

The ticket list has saved views across the top: Open, My open, Unassigned, Waiting on customer, Due soon, AI handed off and All. Each one is applied by the server, so what you see is always what the database holds — there is no separate copy of the list in the page that can drift.

Every account starts with a Standard response promise: eight working hours to answer and two days to resolve, per ticket priority. The ticket list shows the answer deadline as a countdown that turns amber inside the last hour and red once it has passed, and Due soon lists only what is unanswered and due within four hours — soonest deadline first, because that is the order the work has to be done in. A clock is only set when a policy exists: an account with no policy gets an empty deadline rather than a wrong one.

Making your own domain receive mail

Adding a domain gives you a checklist of DNS records. Those records get mail as far as your mail provider. The last step is the one people miss: your provider has to be told where to post what arrives, or the mail sits at the provider and never reaches your inbox here.

Open the domain and press DNS records. Under the records you will find Where your mail provider posts incoming mail with a copy button. Paste that address into your provider as a webhook or route destination — the same address works for every domain, because CoreSwift works out whose mail it is from the address it was sent to.

Next to it you will see whether your signing key is set. This matters more than it sounds: the posting address is public, so anyone could send it a fake message. The signing key is what proves a delivery really came from your provider. Without it, mail reaches us and is refused — which looks like “receiving is broken” when the truth is “nothing proves where it came from”. Your provider shows that key on the domain's own settings page as a short value, distinct from the longer key used for sending, and the screen says plainly which state you are in.

When both are done, send a message from an outside address to one of your mailboxes. If it lands in your inbox, the whole chain is working: DNS, your provider, the posting address, the signing key.

The agent — what it may do on its own, and what waits for you

Your desk has help: an agent that can read a ticket and propose an action. It is off until you switch it on, and it only ever asks for things on a fixed list — leave an internal note, change the priority, tag the customer, add them to a list, hand the ticket to a teammate, or escalate it. An action that is not on that list cannot be proposed at all, so nothing can be invented behind your back.

Two numbers govern money. A per-action limit and a account ceiling, and the smaller one decides: anything above either waits for you, with the reason recorded on the row. A per-action limit can never be set above the ceiling — that would be two numbers where the smaller one decides nothing.

Everything it asks for appears on the Agent screen with Approve and Refuse. Approving carries it out and records the result; a refusal is kept as a record, because "the agent asked for this and was told no" is worth knowing. An action that fails records why rather than disappearing — a silent no-op would be worse than a refusal.

What the AI has cost you — and with whose key

Every AI feature in this account now keeps a tally: which feature called a model, which provider answered, how many tokens went in and out, and whether your own key or the platform's paid for it. The AI panel shows the last thirty days by provider, the most recent calls, and how many ran on the platform key rather than yours.

It also tells you the truth when the AI is off. An empty tally used to read as “free” when the real answer was “no key is set”: the panel now says so plainly, with the field to fix it right underneath. Failed calls are recorded too — a provider refusing a key is exactly the thing worth seeing, and a tally that counted only successes could never show it.

How a customer signs in — and how they get back in

The customer page is your own: your name, your logo and your colour, taken from the same branding you set for your email, so the message they receive and the page they open from it look like one company.

There are two ways in. Email me a sign-in link mails them a link that works once and expires in thirty minutes — nothing to remember. The other way is the email address they wrote in with plus the reference from any of their requests, which stays as the fallback for a account whose mail is not set up yet.

The link carries its one-time token in a part of the address a web server never receives, so it cannot be picked out of a server log, and it is stored only as a scrambled fingerprint — a copy of the database yields no working links. Asking for a link always answers the same way, whether or not that address has ever written in, so the page cannot be used to find out who your customers are.

Finding one ticket

The box above the queue searches the whole account, not just the view you are looking at, because “where is ticket 42” has one answer. Type a ticket number (42, #42 or CS-42), an email address, a name, or a word from the subject, and press Search. Searching an address brings back everything that person has sent, which is usually the real question behind the search.

Is this a new customer or the fifth time this month?

Opening a ticket also shows what that customer sent before: how many earlier tickets, how many of them are still open, and the last five with their status. One click moves to any of them. It reads only your own account, narrowed to the address that wrote in, so it is never somebody else's history — an agent starts an answer knowing whether this is a first question or a repeat.

Files the customer sent

When someone emails a screenshot or a spreadsheet, the file appears on the ticket's timeline under the message that carried it, with its name and size, and one click downloads it. The ticket keeps the link rather than a copy of the bytes — one file, one place — and a file is only ever offered on the ticket whose mail it arrived with.

The numbers at the top of the queue

Above the ticket list is a strip that answers four questions about the last thirty days: how many were raised, how many were solved, what is open now, and what is waiting on you rather than on the customer. Two of them are times — your median first reply and the reply time that nine out of ten tickets beat — so a bad week shows up as a number rather than a feeling. Everything past its promised deadline is counted in red, and those tickets are exactly what the Due soon view holds.

A median with nothing behind it shows a dash, never a zero: a desk nobody has asked anything yet should not look perfect.

Your help centre — one place a customer can look first

Published articles are not only searched from the widget: they make a help centre at /help/your-account, a page a customer can browse without an account. It groups articles into sections — Billing, Getting started, whatever you name — shows the most-read answers, has its own search, and each article has a “did this answer it?” vote. Beneath it sits the same Support chat, so if the answer is not there they can talk to a person without leaving the page.

Sections are managed on the Knowledge Base screen, and every article can be filed into one. An article with no section still publishes and still searches — deleting a section leaves its articles uncategorised rather than deleting them with it.

Saved answers — the same question, answered once

The same question fifty times should cost one answer. Saved answers live on the Knowledge Base page: a title, the text, and an optional shortcut such as /refund. In a ticket, pick one from the list above the reply box and the text is inserted already filled in for that ticket — the customer's first name, the ticket reference, your name and the business name, from the placeholders {{contact.first_name}}, {{ticket.number}}, {{agent.first_name}} and {{site.name}}.

The filling happens on the server, so what you see in the box is exactly what the customer receives. A placeholder the software does not know is left alone rather than blanked, so nothing of yours disappears silently.

When two tickets are one, or one is two

Merge into… folds another ticket's whole conversation into the one you are reading. You give the number to merge into, and that ticket keeps its number — the customer who was told CS-1042 must not find that number now means something else. The other ticket closes and points at its survivor, so it never just vanishes, and the thread records where the extra messages came from.

Split out does the opposite: one message in a long thread that is really its own question becomes a new ticket, with the same customer and the same channel. Only a reply can be split — a note and a system event belong to the ticket they are on.

Both move the conversation rather than copying it, so a customer's words exist in exactly one place at every moment.

Knowledge Base — write the answer once

Knowledge Base holds the answers to the questions your customers keep asking. Each article has a title, which becomes its address, and a plain-language answer. Two switches decide who can read it: Status (a draft is invisible to everyone but you) and Who can read it — Customers means it appears in the widget's search, Team only means it never leaves the console, at any status. A team-only article is knowledge for making a decision, not a page.

Search is built in and works on the words in the title and the answer, so you do not have to tag anything for an article to be findable.

The clock tells you, you don't have to watch it

A background check runs every minute. An hour before a deadline it writes a warning on the ticket's timeline, and when a deadline passes it writes the breach there too — so the clock finds you where you are already reading, once per deadline and never twice, however often the check runs or however many times the software restarts. A ticket that has already been answered cannot breach its answer deadline afterwards, and a solved one cannot breach its resolution deadline. Those events are internal: your customer never sees them.

Replies and notes

Every ticket has two ways to write, and they are deliberately different things. Send reply answers the customer by email. Add internal note records what your team knows — “rang them, invoice already paid”, “third time he has asked” — and never reaches the customer: not by email, not in their portal, not in the status lookup. Notes show on the thread in amber and are labelled as never sent, so nobody mistakes one for a reply.

Because a note does not answer anybody, it also does not stop the first-response clock. That clock starts the first time a person replies.

✉️ Email Compliance & Deliverability

Every message you send leaves a record you can prove

CoreSwift sends on your behalf — receipts, notifications, campaigns, support replies. Three questions matter for every one of them: did it arrive, who asked me to stop, and can I show the evidence. The Mail compliance tab answers all three.

  • Delivery rate — how much of your mail the provider actually delivered.
  • Bounce rate — how much of your list no longer exists. A high number is a list problem, not a sending problem.
  • Complaint rate — how often someone pressed "this is spam". The ceiling is 0.30%; above it, mailbox providers start refusing your mail outright. The figure is shown against that ceiling, never on its own.

These are computed from the delivery reports your provider sends back, as they arrive. Until the first report lands the tab says so plainly instead of showing a tidy zero.

Nobody can be mailed after they ask you to stop

Every non-essential message carries an opt-out link, and that link opens a page with no login to remember: it lists the campaigns that person is actually in, with a tick box beside each, plus one choice to stop everything. One tick on one campaign stops that campaign only — it does not quietly end every other message you send them.

Receipts, password resets and security notices are not stoppable. That is the law and it is also the point: nobody should lose their reset link because they unsubscribed from a newsletter.

An unsubscribe is honoured immediately and remembered permanently — including across a re-imported list, so nobody can be resurrected by accident. Any mail that is known to be dead (a hard bounce) or hostile (a spam complaint) is blocked automatically, for your account only.

Your own words on your own opt-out page

Title, explanation, the stop-everything label, the button, and the two confirmation lines are yours to write, in the Mail compliance tab. Clear a box and that line falls back to the default wording rather than leaving a blank page. Only an owner or an admin can change them.

Give real thought to two of those lines: say plainly what the person is stopping, and make the confirmation say the change is saved. Those two sentences are what turns an annoyed recipient into somebody who stays on your list for the mail they still want.

Take your list with you — and take your blocks with you

Two downloads, both yours alone:

  • Every contact address (CSV) — one address per line, made for pasting into an email-checking service so you can clean a list before you send to it.
  • Do-not-mail list (CSV) — every address that unsubscribed, hard-bounced or complained, with the reason and the date.

You can also add an address by hand, and lift a block you know was wrong.

Your domains — one domain, three lanes

Add a domain once, and then decide which jobs it does. Each lane is separate on purpose, because each carries its own sending reputation and its own DNS records.

  • Inbox — you@yourdomain.com. Real conversations with real people. The warmest lane; protect it.
  • Automation — mg.yourdomain.com. Campaigns and sequences send from here, so a bad blast can never damage the name your replies come from. Its replies point back at your inbox lane, so anyone who answers a campaign reaches a person.
  • Calendar — booking.yourdomain.com. Public booking pages for your customers. Until that name resolves, pages are served from the platform address so nothing is ever dead.

A lane only offers itself once its own records verify. Mail compliance → Your domains shows each lane with its status and the exact DNS records to add: a verified lane reads Verified, and one still waiting says Waiting on records instead of pretending to work. A name you enter must sit under a domain you have already added — the screen will not let an account advertise an address on a domain it does not own.

Sending reputation, and what sharing one means

Sending reputation belongs to a domain. On your own verified domain, your reputation is your own: another account's mistake cannot touch it, and their complaint can never silence your mail.

While you send on the platform's shared address, you share that reputation with everyone else — so the shared address carries a much smaller daily allowance than a domain of your own. That is not a limit on you; it is what stops one careless sender from silencing everybody's password resets. Connect a domain to lift it.

Automation Group Overview

The Automation group (sidebar icon ⚡) contains Rules, Tags and Lead Scoring. The strategy-engine screens this section used to name are not in the console; each card below says what is API-only instead.

The rule builder: when → wait → only if → then

+ New Rule asks four questions and nothing else. Pick when (a tag, a list, a stage, a score, a new contact, a date, or quiet for N days). Optionally set a wait in minutes, hours or days. Optionally tick only if they have NOT replied. Pick then (send a template, add/remove a tag, add/remove from a list, notify a user, call a webhook). Every picker lists your own tags, lists and templates; there is no JSON to type and no free-text id.

Save as draft stores the rule with is_active: false. A draft appears in the list marked Draft, can be paused, resumed or deleted, and is never evaluated by the engine — every rule lookup filters on is_active IS NOT FALSE, so a draft cannot fire, enrol a contact or send a message. Save & activate stores the same rule as active; that is the only control on the screen that arms anything.

What a wait really does. When a delay is set, the match is written to the delayed-action queue with a due time and the engine does not run the action. A background pass picks it up once the time has passed, which is why a "wait three days" step can never send in the same minute the tag was added. The queued row keeps the action as it was when the rule fired, so editing the rule afterwards cannot change what the customer was about to receive.

Case study — the new-lead first follow-up

Situation. A local service business adds every enquiry to the CRM by hand and also receives leads from its directory listing. New enquiries often sit untouched for days, and the owner wanted one follow-up email going out only if nobody had answered within a few hours.

What it was set to. Trigger Contact created (new lead); wait 4 hours; tick only if they have NOT replied; action Send an email template — the workspace's "Thanks for reaching out" template. Saved as a draft first, reviewed, then activated.

What the visitor experienced. Every person who filled in the enquiry form or was added by the directory intake received one email about four hours later — but only when they had not written back in the meantime. A visitor who replied within the hour received nothing further from the rule.

What it produced. One queued delayed-action row per new lead, one outbound message per lead who stayed silent, and a visible count on the rule ("executed N") that the owner could read without opening a log. Contacts who replied left a queued row that recorded why it was skipped, so "did this rule work?" has an answer per contact.

Honest limits. The waiting and the no-reply check are exact; a "reply" is a reply the app can see — inbound mail filed against that contact's address, or a reply event ingested for that contact. A reply typed into some other tool is invisible and the follow-up would still go out. The clock triggers (a date coming up, no activity for N days) are checked every 15 minutes, so they fire within a quarter of an hour of becoming true, never to the second; the renewal date is read from a date field stored on the contact (for example renewal_date). One rule carries one wait and one action: a longer sequence is several rules chained by their own triggers, and each rule's own history is what tells you which step ran.

Webhook Overview

CoreSwift CRM supports both incoming and outgoing webhooks for integration with satellite apps and external systems.

Satellite Apps: FunnelSwift, IncentiveSwift, WorkflowSwift, and MissedCall Respondr can push data into CoreSwift via inbound webhooks.

Inbound Webhooks (Receiving Data)

Other apps can send events to CoreSwift using authenticated webhook endpoints. The URL carries only the key prefix, which is a public index; the credential is the full key in the X-Satellite-Key header, and its SHA-256 digest must equal the digest stored for that prefix. A missing header or a wrong key is refused with 401.

POST /inbound/:key_prefix/:event_type
X-Satellite-Key: <full-api-key>
Content-Type: application/json

{ "payload": "data" }

Or the v2 endpoint:

POST /inbound/v2/:key_prefix/:event_type
X-Satellite-Key: <full-api-key>

And the v3 contact-sync endpoint — POST /inbound/v3/:key_prefix/contact-sync (also survey-complete and contact-upsert) — with the same header.

These three routes are served by the CoreSwift application itself. They are not under /api/, and the public web vhosts proxy only /api/, /s/ and /track/ to it, so on the shared host a satellite app calls CoreSwift directly rather than through the public site.

What arrives. Each delivery is a JSON POST:

Verifying the signature. When the endpoint has a signing secret the X-CoreSwift-Signature header is t=<unix seconds>,v1=<hex>, where v1 is HMAC-SHA256(secret, "<t>.<raw request body>") — compute the same value and reject a mismatch. An endpoint saved without a secret is sent unsigned.

Which events, and retries. An endpoint whose events list is empty receives every event; a non-empty list receives only those event types. An endpoint with is_active: false receives nothing. A failed delivery (anything other than a 2xx) is retried retry_count times with a growing backoff, each attempt bounded by timeout_ms; redirects are never followed. The endpoint's last_triggered_at shows its last successful delivery and failure_count how many deliveries have failed since the last success.

API Keys

Every account has one API key. That single key is the account's identity: the apps that work with CoreSwift (FunnelSwift, WorkflowSwift, IncentiveSwift, ADASwift, MissedCall Respondr) use it to send data in, and an AI client uses the same key for MCP. It has:

  • Prefix The leading characters, shown in the console. It is public and is not a secret.
  • Full Key The credential, sent as Authorization: Bearer <key>. The server stores only its SHA-256 digest (key_hash) and can never recover the key from the row; a sealed copy is kept so you can still view it.
  • Status Active, or gone once you revoke it (a revoked key authenticates nothing).
  • Last Used When it most recently authenticated something.

Exactly one per account. The database refuses a second active key. To replace it press Regenerate: the old key stops working the moment the new one exists, so update whatever was using it. The key is in Settings > Integration Center, and it is mirrored on the MCP page where View & copy shows it after you re-enter your password.

Which way the keys point. Your satellite apps use this key to send data into CoreSwift - CoreSwift does not mint a separate key for each of them. Going the other way, CoreSwift holds each satellite app's own key under its connection settings, so it can reach out to them.

Sending and receiving are two separate choices

You do not have to move your mail to one company. The inbox setup page asks two questions that stand on their own: how should your mail leave (sending) and how should your mail arrive (receiving). Every option in those two lists really works — there is no placeholder in either one.

  • Sending can go through your own Mailgun account, or through any SMTP service — Amazon SES, SendGrid, Postmark, Google Account, or a mail server you run yourself. For those you type the server name, port, username and password that the service gave you; that is the whole setup.
  • Receiving can come through Mailgun or SendGrid, or nowhere at all if the domain only sends.

Mixing them is normal. Sending through Amazon SES while receiving through Mailgun — or sending through Mailgun and receiving through SendGrid — is a supported setup, and it is the one that lets you keep whatever already sends your mail. Changing one never changes the other.

Automated mail leaves from its own address, never your main one. Auto-responders, email sequences and automation-rule emails are sent from mg.yourbusiness.com — a subdomain CoreSwift derives automatically from the domain you added, so there is nothing extra to set up. That keeps a complaint about an automated blast away from the address your real conversations arrive on. Replies still reach you: every automated message is sent with your own inbox as the reply address, so a customer who answers lands back in your inbox.

Receiving through SendGrid

  1. Add the domain on the setup page and choose Receiving: SendGrid. No SendGrid key is needed: SendGrid posts each message to an address we give you.
  2. At your DNS host add one MX record pointing your domain at mx.sendgrid.net with priority 10. If your domain is on Cloudflare, leave it DNS only (grey cloud).
  3. Copy the SendGrid Inbound Parse address the setup page shows you — ours, ending in a long private code — then in SendGrid open Settings → Inbound Parse, add your domain and paste it as the destination.
  4. Press Check again. The receiving group turns Active once your domain’s mail really points at SendGrid, and that is when the inbox can be created.

That address is a password. SendGrid’s Inbound Parse signs nothing, so the address itself is the only thing proving a delivery is genuinely yours: anyone holding it can post mail into your inbox. Keep it out of tickets and screenshots, and never put it on a page.

Your sending records stay yours. When you receive through SendGrid, the SPF and DKIM records remain exactly as your sending service gave them — Amazon SES mints its own, SendGrid mints its own, Mailgun mints its own. The setup page says where they come from instead of inventing them. If the same domain also sends through Mailgun, Mailgun’s own sending records are shown, because they are yours and we can read them.

Receiving can be changed without touching sending. The setup page shows Change how this domain receives under the domain; pick Mailgun, SendGrid or Nowhere. Your own Mailgun account: the two keys, and where to copy each one

Two keys, two different jobs, and neither is a password you use to sign in here. You paste both once, on the inbox setup page; the app stores them sealed (encrypted) and uses them only for your own mail. Both are copied from your own Mailgun account.

  • The API key is the sending key. It is what lets CoreSwift hand your outgoing mail to your Mailgun account, and ask Mailgun what became of it — delivered, bounced, marked as spam. Where to copy it: sign in at Mailgun, open Settings → API Keys, and copy the Private API key (it begins with key-). One key belongs to the whole Mailgun account, so a second domain on the same account can reuse it.
  • The webhook signing key is the receiving key. Mailgun signs every message it forwards to you with this key, and CoreSwift checks that signature before it accepts the message, so nobody can post fake mail into your inbox. Without it a domain can only send: mail addressed to it is refused, which is why the setup step asks for it before it will create an inbox. Where to copy it: Mailgun, Send → Domains → your domain → Webhook signing key — a different value for each domain, unlike the API key.

A wrong or rotated key is visible, not silent. Paste a new value at any time on the same screen; the old one stops being used the moment it is saved. If Mailgun stops accepting a key, that domain’s badge reads Needs attention with a sentence saying what Mailgun answered, and the rest of the app keeps working.

Storage: the meter, the three warnings, and what a full mailbox does

Every account has a storage allowance, and it grows with the plan. Free starts at 2 GB; Starter 10 GB, Professional 50 GB, Enterprise 100 GB and Agency 250 GB. (Pro and the Inbox line carry 10 GB and 5 GB in the catalogue but are not on sale, so they are not part of this ladder.) Storage counts the mail you have received and sent, plus the logo and profile pictures you upload.

The meter is on the mail screen all the time, at the bottom of the left-hand column, and it states the real figures — for example “1.5 of 2 GB used (75%)” — never just “storage”.

You are warned three times, and at most once per level per month. At 50%, 75% and 99% full a banner appears above the message list and stays there until space is freed. The account owner also gets one email per level per month, carrying the same numbers, what to do, and a link to the mail screen and the plans. The same level is never emailed twice in one month.

When the reminders arrive. The figures are checked whenever you open your mail, and again in the background every ten minutes, so a mailbox nobody opens is still warned by email. One email per level per month is the most you will ever receive — the same level cannot be mailed twice in one month, however much mail arrives in between.

A full mailbox never loses mail. At 100% plain mail still arrives and is stored, sending still works, and nothing is deleted to make room. The only thing that pauses is adding a new attachment or file (for example uploading a logo): that answers with a sentence naming the figures and the two ways out — delete or archive mail (Trash and Spam count toward the figure until emptied), or upgrade for more storage. Everything works again the moment the figure drops below the allowance.

Time-based retention is only a far backstop. Mail is not removed on a clock: the default backstop is ten years (3,650 days), and the mail screen always states the window that applies to the account, together with the date the oldest message would reach it. Freeing space is always your choice — by deleting or archiving — never an automatic purge to make room.

Seeing a domain's DNS records, and removing a domain you no longer want

Every domain on Email Domains & Sending has two buttons beside its verification badge.

  • DNS records asks your own mail provider for the exact values that domain needs — the receiving MX records, the SPF text record and the DKIM key — and shows them in a table with a Copy button on every value. Add each one at your DNS host (Cloudflare: DNS → Records), then press Re-check. Mail records stay DNS only (grey cloud); only the branded inbox CNAME is proxied. This works on every domain at any time, including the second and third domain you add to an existing account.
  • Remove this domain (inside the DNS records window) deletes a domain you no longer want — and the inboxes that live on it. Mail already received stays in your Inbox; only the addresses go away. Because that cannot be undone, the app asks you to type the domain name to confirm, and refuses every other answer.

24 Round robin: who gets the next lead

Where it lives

Open the Round Robin tab. It is part of the Enterprise and Agency plans; on a plan without it the screen says the module is not on your current plan rather than showing an empty list.

A team, and the two ways it hands work out

Press + New Team, give it a name and choose a strategy:

  • Round robin (take turns) — the teammate who has waited longest gets the next one.
  • Least busy (fewest open bookings) — whoever currently holds the fewest open assignments, and never more than that person’s max concurrent bookings.

Those are the only two the assignment engine implements. The screen used to also offer Weighted and Random, and pressing one built a team every assignment was then refused for — so a team made earlier with one of those two shows the reason on its own card and nothing else. Delete it and make it again with one of the two above.

Adding people

Press + Add member on a team and pick the person from the list — the list is this account’s own roster, by name and address, and only active people are offered. (It used to ask you to paste an internal user id; the roster was always available to read, so that box is gone.) Each member carries a weight, which is kept on the record but is not used by either strategy above, and a max concurrent bookings limit, which is what caps the least-busy strategy.

Take someone out with Remove; their past assignments stay in the history.

What happened, and turning a team off

Assignments on a team lists every hand-out with the teammate, the contact, the time and the status (pending, accepted, rejected, completed). A team can be switched off without deleting it: its card shows off and it stops being offered, while its history stays readable. Deleting a team removes its members and its assignment history with it, so switch it off if you may want the record.

Before a mail feature will work: what it needs

Three different identities send and receive your mail, and each one is set up separately. If one is missing, the feature that needs it stops and tells you why — it does not fail quietly.

  • Ordinary mail — receipts, invitations, password resets, and your replies. It leaves as noreply@yourdomain. Until you verify your own domain it uses the shared platform identity, which means your customer sees our name rather than yours.
  • Automation mail — anything a rule, a sequence or a campaign sends. It leaves as mg.yourdomain, kept separate on purpose so a large send cannot damage the reputation of the address your receipts use. It cannot send at all until your domain is verified, because there is no base domain to build the subdomain from.
  • Inbound mail — the shared inbox you read and reply from. It needs your domain and the inbound signing key, because a delivery that cannot be authenticated is refused.

The inbound signing key, and why it is 32 characters

Receiving mail needs one credential: the HTTP webhook signing key from your provider — Settings → API Security → HTTP webhook signing key, press Show. It is 32 hexadecimal characters and it is account-wide: one value covers every domain in the account.

It is not your sending API key. That one is 50 characters with dashes in the middle, and pasting it here is the single most common cause of an inbox that looks empty — the panel used to accept it and then every delivery was refused on arrival. It is now refused at the door with that explanation.

Before you paste a key, receiving already works on the platform credential this app carries. Pasting the correct key upgrades it: each delivery is then authenticated against your own key, which is the stronger door. A wrong key is worse than no key — with none, mail arrives; with a wrong one, everything is refused — so the panel offers Remove key as well.

How to set any of them up: Email Domains → add your domain → add the records the page shows you → press Re-check until it reads verified → press Set key and paste your provider's HTTP webhook signing key (mailgun → Settings → API Security). The posting address for inbound mail is on the same page.

When you open Campaigns before that is done, the screen is replaced by this setup with the exact reason — and nothing is sent or queued while it is stopped. Once the lane is ready the feature opens normally.

Is your booking page actually bookable?

Open the calendar and the top of the screen answers one question: can a customer book this now? When the answer is no it lists, in order, exactly what is missing and what to press:

  • A bookable resource — a position a customer can pick. With none, the public page opens and offers nothing, which looks to a visitor like a broken site.
  • Opening hours (appointment calendars) or open positions ahead (dated-slot and date-range calendars) — otherwise the calendar offers no times at all.
  • A tag or a list on confirm — optional for selling, but this is what lets a booking reach your automations. Without it the booking is recorded and nothing else happens.

When all three are satisfied the card reads "A customer can book this now", and the public link below it is the one to publish or embed.

The bookings your customers made

Under Settings → Bookings taken (right beside Booking) is the list of real bookings, not the calendar setup: who booked, for which dates, on which calendar, in what state, and what they paid. Cancel on a row puts the slot back on the calendar.

A booking is already confirmed the moment it is made — the public page takes the slot and holds it, so there is no separate "accept" step and no button for one. The state column reads confirmed or cancelled, and a cancelled booking frees its place for the next customer.

If the list is empty, no one has booked yet: share the calendar link from Settings → Booking and the first booking appears here.

Deal fields — the details beyond amount and stage

Every business records something a generic deal form cannot know: a drop-off city, a reference number, a delivery date. Deals → Deal fields is where you add them.

  • Press + Add a field, name it, and choose a type: text, number, money, date, yes/no, or pick from a list.
  • The key shown next to each field is what an AI agent or an import uses to address it, and it is made from the name so it stays predictable.
  • Remove stops a field appearing on new deals. Values already recorded on existing deals are left alone.

Deal fields belong to your account, so two businesses in the same place can record completely different details.

Alert rules — what CoreSwift does while you are not looking

The notifications list shows what happened. Notifications → Alert rules is what decides it: each rule is "when this happens, do that".

  • When this happens is the event name the system publishes — for example deal.stalled, contact.created, booking.confirmed.
  • Do this is one of four things: put it in this feed, send an email, send a text message, or send a WhatsApp message.
  • Pause keeps a rule but stops it firing; Remove deletes it. Nothing already sent is affected either way.
  • Waiting to go out lists what the server has queued and not yet delivered — the honest answer to "why have I not had that email yet".

Score destinations — where a lead score goes

Lead scoring gives each contact a number. Lead Scoring → Score destinations is where that number is delivered when it changes — your own automation, an n8n workflow, a Slack hook.

  • Each destination has a name, a web address, and the score range that should trigger it (at least this score, and optionally at most that one).
  • Leave this empty and scores stay inside CoreSwift, visible only on the Lead Scoring screen.
  • A signing secret is optional and stored encrypted; it travels with the call so the receiving end can prove the request came from you.

Hand a lead to the team

The Round Robin screen lists your teams and how leads rotate between their members. The button that actually does it is Hand a lead to the team at the top.

Pick a team, optionally name the contact, and press Assign now — round robin gives the lead to the next person in turn, so the work spreads instead of landing on whoever answers first. With no contact named the lead is assigned without one; a team is required, because the rotation belongs to the team.

Assign a ticket to a teammate

On Tickets, the Assigned to cell is a button for an owner or admin. Press it, pick a teammate, and the conversation belongs to them. Choose Leave it unassigned to put it back in the shared queue.

A teammate sees only their own work. Somebody invited as a member signs in to the tickets that are assigned to them — not the whole desk — and a ticket that is not theirs answers "not found" rather than showing a conversation that belongs to someone else. If a member sees an empty queue, nothing is broken: nothing has been assigned to them yet.

Day and night, on every page and every address

Every CoreSwift page has a switch, and the choice is remembered across all of them: press it once on the booking page and the console, the sign-in screen, the inbox and the guides are all that theme too, whichever CoreSwift address you are on.

  • In the console: the moon icon in the header, or Day / night in the sidebar.
  • Every other page, including this guide: the small switch in the bottom-left corner.
  • It is remembered in a small cookie on coreswiftcrm.com — the only store every subdomain can read — with your browser's own storage as a backup. Nothing about it is stored on our side, and until you choose, it follows your computer's light or dark setting.
  • If your browser refuses cookies, the switch still works; the choice just stays with the address you set it on.

An account and a workspace are not the same thing

An account is the business — it is what you signed up for, what your plan belongs to, and what your data is separated by. A workspace lives inside an account, for splitting one business's work (by branch, brand or team) without a second login.

Two consequences worth knowing:

  • Switching a workspace changes what you are looking at, never who you are — your plan, your people and your billing stay with the account.
  • An account always keeps at least one workspace, and an account that is one of our portfolio businesses keeps its workspaces permanently. In that case the Delete button is shown disabled, with the reason on hover, instead of failing when you press it.
